Where we are: two things to model

Every longitudinal model so far has made two choices: one for the mean, one for the covariance.

Mean Covariance In R
Linear regression (M1–M2) Covariates \(\sigma^2 I\): independence lm()
RM-ANOVA (M3) One mean per occasion, one group Sphericity / compound symmetry aov(y ~ t + Error(id/t))
Response profiles (M4) Group × occasion (time as factor) Unstructured gls(..., corSymm, varIdent)
Parametric curves (M5) Group × \(f(t)\): line, quadratic, spline Unstructured gls(y ~ group * t, ...)
Covariance patterns (M6.1) Saturated (kept fixed) CS, AR(1), exponential… gls(..., corAR1 / corExp)
Mixed models (M6.2) \(X\beta\) (population) + \(Zb_i\) (subject) Induced: \(ZGZ' + \sigma^2 I\) lmer(y ~ t + (1 + t | id))

Mixed models are the only row where the covariance comes from a mechanism (subjects have their own curves) and therefore works with any timing of measurements.

From questions to tools

The model is the means. The scientific question is answered with contrasts and plots.

Question Tool
What is the mean response at a given time? emmeans(m, ~ time, at = ...)
How fast does it change? emtrends(m, ~ 1, var = "time")
From which time does it differ from baseline? contrast(emmeans(m, ~ time), "trt.vs.ctrl")
What does the model look like? ggeffects::ggpredict() + plot()
Is the model reasonable? performance::check_model(), icc(), r2()
How sure are we about σ’s or any derived number? confint(method = "boot"), bootMer(), PBmodcomp()
Adding a group: do groups differ? At which times? emmeans(m, ~ group | time) + pairs() / contrast()
Do groups change at a different rate? emtrends(m, ~ group, var = "time")
Is the change different between groups? contrast(..., interaction = c(group = ..., time = ...))

Numbers (simulated): pooled lm() ≈ +0.24; between people ≈ +0.35; within each person ≈ −0.50.

Between: a person who types 1 wpm faster on average makes 0.35 more errors. Within: when a person types 1 wpm faster than their own average, they make 0.5 fewer errors (because he improves!). The pooled slope mixes both.

A mixed model can separate them (each person’s mean speed + deviation from it); just mention it.

Same mean profile, different stories

The thick line is the same in both panels: a table of means per visit, or the time coefficient of a fixed-effects-only model, cannot tell A from B.

  • A: everybody improves by about the same amount, so the average effect describes each person well -> random intercept is enough: slopes are almost equal.
  • B: half improve a lot (≈ 4 per visit), half not at all. The average effect (≈ 2 per visit) happens to nobody -> the random-slope variance is huge, and the fanning lines make the variance grow over time (heteroscedasticity).
  • Pedir que miren primero solo las líneas negras: “Same study?”
  • A → con intercepto aleatorio basta. B → varianza de la pendiente aleatoria enorme; el abanico hace crecer la varianza con el tiempo (heterocedasticidad de M3).
  • Mensaje clínico: en B hay respondedores y no respondedores; el efecto medio no le pasa a nadie.
  • Pregunta: “Which one would you rather have as a patient?”

When the mean profile lies: drop-out

Simulated: patients leave once their marker exceeds 62 (informative drop-out).

Everybody keeps getting worse, but the sickest leave first, so the observed mean flattens out and badly underestimates the decline. Always plot who is measured when.

  • Question, looking only at the red line: “Does the disease stabilise after visit 4?”
  • Informative drop-out: the sickest leave first.
  • One line: mixed models (likelihood-based) use all available visits → fine if drop-out depends on what was observed (MAR, Missing At Random); not if it depends on what was not observed (MNAR, Missing Not At Random).
  • Always report n per visit.

Checklist: plots before any longitudinal model

  1. Spaghetti plot: all subjects, or a random sample if there are many. Do lines fan out? Parallel?
  2. Mean profile ± SE per group, with n per time: who is still there?
  3. Facet by group, and color by subject when there are few.
  4. Choose the time axis: time since baseline, age, time since an event?
  5. Missingness pattern: when do people stop being measured, and are they different?

Model what you have seen, not what you assumed.

The compromise: partial pooling

Each person has their own line, but those lines are assumed to be similar: they are drawn from the same population (with individual deviations around an average line: each person’s line borrows strength from everyone else’s). Below: i= drivers, j = Days

\[ \text{Reaction}_{ij} = (\beta_1 + b_{1i}) + (\beta_2 + b_{2i})\,\text{Days}_{ij} + \varepsilon_{ij},\qquad \begin{pmatrix} b_{1i}\\ b_{2i}\end{pmatrix} \sim N(0, G), ~ G = \begin{pmatrix} \operatorname{Var}(b_{1i}) & \operatorname{Cov}(b_{1i}, b_{2i}) \\ \operatorname{Cov}(b_{1i}, b_{2i}) & \operatorname{Var}(b_{2i}) \end{pmatrix} \]

  • \(\beta_1, \beta_2\): population line (fixed effects).
  • \(b_{1i}, b_{2i}\): how person \(i\) deviates from it (random effects).
  • \(G\): how much people vary (σ’s and their correlation).
  • Mapear con M6.2 (notación FLW: β₁, β₂; G y R).
  • Pregunta: “How many parameters in G?” (3: dos varianzas + covarianza).
  • “Borrow strength”: conductores con datos ruidosos se acercan a la media → diapo de shrinkage.

Formula Meaning Implied \(G\)
(1 | Subject) random intercept only: parallel lines \(g_{11}\) only (compound symmetry, M3)
(1 + Days | Subject) random intercept + slope, correlated full \(2 \times 2\)
(Days || Subject) random intercept + slope, independent diagonal, \(g_{12} = 0\)

Outside the brackets: what is common to everybody. Inside: what varies (left of |) and by whom (right of |). Read | as “per”.

The bootstrap

When the formulas are not enough.

Why? Three cracks in the usual inference

  1. No exact df for fixed effects: Satterthwaite/KR are approximations, shaky with few subjects (18 here).
  2. Variance components (σ’s) are skewed and cannot be negative → Wald CIs are bad.
  3. “Do I need the random slope?” tests whether a variance \(g_{22} = 0\), on the boundary, but a variance cannot be negative → the usual χ² p-value is then too large (conservative)

Parametric bootstrap: the idea

No df, no normal approximation for θ̂: the sampling distribution is simulated from the fitted model. θ can be a σ, a prediction, a contrast, an ICC…

Any derived quantity: bootMer()

Example: estimate the % of drivers who worsen by more than 20 ms/day (personal slope), and how uncertain that number is. Why bootstrap? This % is not a parameter but a combination of \(\hat\beta_2\) and \(\hat\sigma\). There is no formula for its SE; the bootstrap gets it by simulation.

# % of drivers worsening > 20 ms/day
pct_fast <- function(fit) {
  b <- fixef(fit)["Days"]
  vc <- VarCorr(fit) |> as_tibble()
  s  <- vc$sdcor[which(vc$var1 == "Days")]
  100 * (1 - pnorm(20, b, s))
}
set.seed(2026)
bt_sl <- bootMer(m_ind, pct_fast, nsim = 1000)
bt_sl$t0
#> [1] 11.02
quantile(bt_sl$t, c(.025, .975))
#>    2.5%   97.5% 
#>  0.3958 27.8685

pct_fast() computes it from a fitted model:

  • b: average slope (11.44 ms/day).
  • s: SD of slopes across drivers (6.99).

bootMer(m_ind, pct_fast, nsim = 1000): simulates 1000 new studies of 18 drivers from the model, refits each one and recomputes `pct_fast()’. Result: 1000 percentages.

The resampling unit is the subject, not the observation.
